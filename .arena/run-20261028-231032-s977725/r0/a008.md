# The Mirror: pitch review

## Assumptions and open questions resolved

I’m reviewing the supplied script and the matching slide notes in `index.html`; I assume the running demo supports the described controls and local camera fallback. The assignment fixes the five time slots and asks for four speakers, so the timing recommendations keep those slots. No judging rubric is stated, so these are likely audience/judge questions, not invented scoring criteria.

The open questions are whether the joke lands quickly, the demo is credible and safe, the pricing reads as fiction, the opening earns its time, four handoffs fit, and the ending is memorable. The script mostly answers these; rehearsal and clear on-screen labels are what make those answers convincing.

## What is working

The premise is legible: a phone app sells access to the mirror the phone already has. The Silver-to-Gold switch demonstrates the joke instead of only describing it, and the reveal keeps the monochrome premium launch tone. The script also explicitly protects the essentials: optional camera permission, no recording or upload, fictional download and upgrade claims, a dismissible deletion-fee gag, and a free exit.

## Likely objections and risk

- **“Is this a real app or just a pitch deck?” High risk.** The script calls it a live demo, but the close spends time restating the concept instead of naming what the team actually implemented. Show the running Silver/Gold controls changing the preview; state that the fallback works without permission. Say “We built a working mirror app with Silver and Gold display modes” only if that is accurate.
- **“What happens to the camera feed?” High risk for trust.** “Temporary and local” is good but abstract. At the permission prompt, say: “The preview stays on this device while the screen is open; we don’t record, upload, or retain it.” Ensure the camera stops when leaving the demo. Keep this line brief and factual; privacy should reassure, not become a second product pitch.
- **“Could those prices or the deletion fee actually charge me or block me?” High risk if the UI is ambiguous.** The site labels the download as fictional and the Gold CTA as mock only; make the ₹5,000 extras action equally unmistakably mock. Keep “No purchase happens” adjacent to the pricing gag. If showing the deletion-fee dialog, make “joke” and “CLOSE FOR FREE” immediately visible, and demonstrate that dismissing it or exiting is straightforward. The joke should never delay or prevent closing.
- **“Why spend 30 seconds on the intro?” Medium risk.** The water-to-phone film can establish the serious reveal, but a history montage can feel like filler before a very simple app. Keep it silent, visually changing, and purposeful; land the title at 25 seconds and transition immediately. The film earns its slot only if it is polished and rehearsed.
- **“Will the four speakers fit?” Medium risk.** The structure is clear, but a demo toggle, pause, and handoff eat into the 2:30 speaking time. The close repeats privacy, file sizes, and fee details already covered. Rehearse to the clock; trim those repeats before cutting the Silver/Gold demonstration or the final line.

**Suggested tighter close:** “We built a working mirror app with Silver and Gold modes, to show how a basic feature gets dressed up as premium software. The camera preview is temporary and local; nothing is recorded, uploaded, or retained. The fees and download claims are jokes, and closing is always free. We made a mirror for a phone that already had one. That’s the point. Thank you.”

**What did you build, and why is it useless?** “We built a working phone mirror with a free partial-screen Silver mode and a mock ₹2,000 full-screen Gold upgrade. It’s useless because the same phone already has a front camera—and Gold only sells you the rest of your screen.”

## Synthesis

The strongest case for the current script is that it sustains the serious launch voice long enough for the escalating pricing to reveal the satire, while the demo and safeguards make the concept tangible. The strongest counterpoint is that 30 seconds of film plus repeated explanatory lines can crowd out the proof and flatten the ending. Keep the cinematic opening and premium voice, but make the live mode switch the centerpiece, state the privacy and mock-payment facts once where they matter, and finish with the concise built/useless answer. That preserves the bit while answering the practical objections.
